Module II

Good, Evil, and the Fading Light

Core Question: Is objective moral clarity possible in an age of relativism?

In a modern world, moral relativism is often the default setting. We are taught that "tolerance" means treating all ethics as opinion. But when we lose the ability to declare acts objectively wrong, the light of moral clarity fades into a gray fog where anything can be justified under the right "situation."

The Logic of duty: Immanuel Kant

Immanuel Kant was the ultimate anti-relativist. He argued that morality is a matter of logical duty, not feelings or consequences. He gave us the Categorical Imperative: "Act only according to that rule that you would want to become a Universal Law for everyone." If a rule cannot be universalized without society collapsing, it is logically wrong. Kant also gave us the Formula of Humanity: never treat another human being merely as a "means" to an end, but always as an "end" in themselves, possessing absolute value.

The Profile: Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)

The human clock: Kant lived his entire life in Königsberg, Prussia. He was so disciplined that the citizens set their watches by his 3:30 PM daily walk. He believed that the ultimate human freedom was autonomy—governing yourself through your own reason, refusing to let kings, priests, or crowds think for you. To Kant, you are only a free agent when you obey the Universal Moral Law because your own reason declares it right.

The Antidotes to Relativism

To resist the fading light, we must recover our moral chest and muscles:

  • C.S. Lewis — In The Abolition of Man, Lewis warns against turning into "Men Without Chests"—intellectuals who have dismissed objective values ("The Tao") as mere feelings, leaving themselves paralyzed and easily ruled by authority.
  • Aristotle — Virtue is not an intellectual concept; it is a habit. You do not think your way to being good; you practice it until it becomes muscle memory.
  • Alasdair MacIntyre — Modern moral language is fragmented and broken. We use words like "good" and "just" without a cohesive story to give them meaning, leading to endless, unresolvable arguments.

Key Takeaway

Morality is not a subjective feeling or a social construct; it is a rational duty and a practiced skill. It requires an internal compass, not a crowd-pleasing radar.

Recommended Readings

  • Immanuel Kant — Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals
  • C.S. Lewis — The Abolition of Man
  • Aristotle — Nicomachean Ethics
  • Alasdair MacIntyre — After Virtue
